# Stakeholders — water as public good for whom

The phrase "water as public good" hides massive asymmetry. Water in India is rivalrous (my litre means less for you) and increasingly excludable (private borewells, tankers, packaged water). It is not a textbook public good. It is a common-pool resource quietly privatized through extraction rights, and the people who experience it as a "public good" are mostly those with assets — land to dig under, taps that work, tariffs they can absorb.

So "public good for whom" splits into stakeholders with radically different stakes. The system today serves the loudest voices at the cost of the quietest. The composition of who is in the room mirrors the asymmetric failure mode.

## Who is in the room

These stakeholders show up in formal water governance — water bureaucracies, allocation tribunals, scheme committees, scientific agencies, advisory bodies, courts, philanthropic decision-making.

**Government water bureaucracies.** IMD, CWC, CGWB, CPCB, NMCG, MoJS at the Centre; State Water Departments, State PCBs, State Groundwater Authorities at state level. Generators of data, allocators of resource. Optimize for departmental KPIs and political compatibility. Cross-agency turf protection is structural.

**Hydrological researchers.** IIT (Roorkee, Madras, Bombay), IISc, NIH (Roorkee), ATREE, ICRISAT, IIM-A, Wadia Institute (Himalayan), CWRDM (Kerala). Analyze. Largely academic; influence on operational decisions is modest.

**Civic intermediaries — small but present.** IndiaWaterPortal (Arghyam), SANDRP (South Asia Network on Dams Rivers People), WELL Labs, Veditum, CSE/Down to Earth, ATREE, ACWADAM, Centre for Water and Sanitation (CEPT), 2030 Water Resources Group, Climate Risk Horizons. Repackage, interpret, advocate. Under-resourced relative to scale.

**Philanthropy.** Arghyam (Rohini Nilekani; the dominant water-only India funder, ~₹50 cr/year scale, since 2005). Tata Water Mission. HCL Foundation. HSBC Water Programme. Reliance Foundation. Coca-Cola Foundation (controversial). 2030 WRG. Rainmatter Foundation (water-relevant subset). Edelgive. Bezos Earth Fund (international, India-relevant).

**International development.** World Bank, UNESCO, IWMI (Colombo HQ, India focus), ICIMOD (Kathmandu, Himalayan basin focus), USAID, GIZ, FCDO. Capital + comparative knowledge.

**Investigative journalists.** A small subset — Mongabay India, The Third Pole, Down to Earth, Scroll's water beat, CSE coverage, regional water reporters. Shape elite + diaspora opinion. Not enough to create durable political demand.

**Activist networks.** Tarun Bharat Sangh (Rajendra Singh, Rajasthan), Paani Foundation (Maharashtra), watershed sangathans (multiple), Ganga Action Parivar, Yamuna Bachao, Cauvery Calling (Isha Foundation, controversial), Narmada Bachao Andolan (Medha Patkar; defining anti-dam movement since 1985), FANSA (drinking water + sanitation), Plachimada (Coca-Cola Kerala, 2002+), Mehdiganj (Coca-Cola UP). These movements have shaped policy more than schemes have. Treated as opposition rather than as governance signal.

**Legal practitioners.** NGT track (since 2010). Environmental lawyers. SC public-interest litigators. MC Mehta cases on water, ~30 years. Vellore Citizens Welfare Forum (1996), Subhash Kumar v Bihar (1991). Judicial activism is the de facto enforcement layer in the absence of legislative reform.

**Industry water risk officers.** Only the largest corporates with international exposure (Tata, ITC, HUL, Mahindra, Coca-Cola, PepsiCo). Risk officers know more about Indian water risk via reinsurance models than the Indian state does. Knowledge proprietary.

## Who is structurally absent

The list is longer than the list above and structurally consequential.

**Citizens / voters.** Water is invisible as a political issue except in acute crises. There is no durable water-vote-bank in any state. Politicians don't compete on water performance because voters don't measure it. This is the foundational absence; everything else flows from it.

**Farmers.** Most affected, least informed. ~120 million Indian farm households operate essentially outside the formal water-information system. Decisions on crop choice, pumping, scheduling are based on local knowledge and word-of-mouth. Free electricity removes the price feedback that would close any decision loop.

**Urban informal settlements.** Most water-precarious population (~17-30% of urban population, depending on definition). Pay 10× per litre via tankers, vendors, packaged water. Zero representation in any formal water decision body. Climate stress will widen the gap; when the next Day Zero arrives, gated communities will have private wells + sumps + tanker contracts and informal settlements will not.

**Women.** ~80% of household water collection labour in rural India. Median collection time 2-3 hours/day in stressed regions. Water collection routes are documented sites of gender-based violence. Open defecation forces women into night-time exposure to assault. Menstrual hygiene + water access is linked. Yet women own ~14% of agricultural land — they don't own the wells they depend on. Decision-making bodies (Water User Associations, panchayat water committees) are male-dominated. Women carry water; men allocate it.

**Children.** Roughly 35% of under-5 Indian children are stunted. WASH absence is a major contributor — diarrhea kills, repeat illness blunts growth. School toilet absence drives female dropout past puberty. Children + water + human capital is one chain; almost no policy framework treats it as one chain.

**Dalit / caste-excluded communities.** Caste-based water exclusion is technically illegal since 1955 (Untouchability Offences Act) and 1989 (Atrocities Act). It is socially active. Mahad Satyagraha (Ambedkar, 1927) was a water protest — Dalit drinking from a public tank. NCRB data still shows water access as one of the most common village-level atrocity triggers. Manual scavenging — caste-bound labour of cleaning sewers and septic tanks — kills hundreds of workers per year, almost entirely Dalit. Sanitation worker deaths underreported. Water and sewage are mirror systems and both are caste-coded.

**Tribal / Adivasi communities.** Live on watersheds. Steward forests + springsheds + small streams customarily. Allocate nothing in formal systems. Forest Rights Act 2006 recognized prior rights to forests; there is no equivalent FRA for water. Customary water rights have no formal standing. Polavaram, Sardar Sarovar, Maheshwar — tribal displacement under dam projects is ongoing and contested.

**Coastal fishers and fishing communities.** Livelihood depends on water as medium (fish, salt, mangroves). Affected by upstream dams (sediment depletion, river fragmentation), barrages, port construction, pollution, sea-level rise, salinity. Marginal in formal water decisions despite ~20 million Indians in fisheries.

**Downstream users.** Whoever sits below — riparian state, downstream village, delta community, Bangladesh on Teesta, Pakistan on Indus. Dependent on upstream restraint. No principled framework for compensation or just sharing. Just political muscle and tribunal orders.

**Future generations.** Aquifers that took 1000 years to fill are being drained in one. Glaciers built over ice ages are melting in decades. Future generations will inherit the bill but have zero standing in current allocation. Standard cost-benefit analysis discounts them at 5-7% per year, which values them at near-zero. Intergenerational standing as a legal concept is emerging globally (Netherlands Urgenda 2019, German Constitutional Court 2021) but not in India.

**Non-human users.** Rivers as ecosystems, wetlands, riparian flora, fish, deltas, migratory waterbirds (80+ Ramsar sites are flyway nodes), Gangetic dolphin (population <3,000), gharial (Chambal-confined, critically endangered), mahseer (collapsed by dam fragmentation), smooth-coated otter, Sundarbans tiger. Uttarakhand HC declared Ganga + Yamuna "living entities" in 2017; SC stayed within months. No legal personhood. e-Flow regulation exists on paper. Non-human users have no standing in any allocation tribunal.

**Sanitation workers.** Manual scavengers, sewer cleaners, septic-tank cleaners, water-tanker drivers, plumbers, RO mechanics, bottled-water delivery, irrigation operators. The bodies that carry the system. Almost entirely Dalit, contractual, informal. Labour rights weakly enforced. Deaths underreported. Politically voiceless.

**Religious institutions.** Mosques (zamzam ethos, ablution facilities), temples (sacred tank tradition), churches (WASH programs through Catholic and Protestant networks), gurdwaras (langar + sarovar), Buddhist water rituals, Jain water-life ethics. Vast cultural-water capital; rich tradition of water stewardship in the cultural register. Almost zero translation to formal water governance. The Ganga is sacred and is the most polluted river. Cauvery is Kaveri Amma. Yamuna is goddess at Yamunotri and sewer in Delhi. The cultural frame and the governance frame have never been bridged.

**Schools and curriculum bodies.** Water literacy at every level is poor. Most urban schoolchildren don't know where their water comes from or where their wastewater goes. Hydrological imagination is a civic literacy gap.

**Local panchayats.** Formally responsible for water in many states under decentralization. Structurally under-capacitated, under-funded, often captured by upper-caste / dominant-caste elite within villages. The Atal Bhujal scheme works at GP level, which is the right unit for some interventions and the wrong unit for aquifer governance.

**Insurance industry.** Should be central to water risk transfer. Marginal in India. Crop insurance pays poorly. Flood insurance penetration is <5%. Aquifer-collapse insurance does not exist (perhaps cannot, economically). Without insurance, there is no risk price signal, and capital flows into water-stressed real estate without correction.

**Urban planners.** Master plans rarely water-grounded. Bengaluru apartments built where water doesn't reach. Hyderabad expanding into Krishna basin pressure zones. Real estate priced without water-stress projection. Climate-water risk in property markets nascent at best.

**Climate adaptation community.** Siloed from the water community despite obvious overlap. Climate finance flows globally — Green Climate Fund, Adaptation Fund — but India's water-climate adaptation finance architecture is thin. Loss and damage accounting in India is post-hoc, partial.

**Health system.** Primary health centres in fluoride/arsenic zones don't have screening. Disease surveillance via IDSP captures fragments. Water-borne disease incidence is among the largest mass-poisoning records in human history (~50M arsenic-exposed, ~60M+ fluoride-exposed) and is not surveilled in real time. The water-health linkage is invisible to both water bureaucracy and health system.

**Diaspora capital + tech.** Indian diaspora has nostalgic interest, financial means, technical capability. Compare with Israeli diaspora's role in Israel's water tech. Mostly untapped.

**Border / transboundary communities.** Affected by upstream Chinese decisions on Yarlung Tsangpo (Brahmaputra). Affected by Indian decisions on Indus (Pakistan), Teesta (Bangladesh), Mahakali (Nepal). No voice in any forum that decides.

## The structural pattern

The list of who is in the room versus who is structurally absent is not random. It tracks political consequence.

People with political voice (urban middle class, industry, paddy farmers in the GW belt with electoral leverage, state water bureaucracies) are heard. People who carry the costs (women, Dalits, tribals, urban informal, downstream, future, non-human) are not. Federalism is a structural shield that protects state-level water mismanagement from federal accountability.

This is the diagnosis: **water-as-public-good will get built when the affected become politically consequential, OR when an acute crisis aligns urban middle-class interest with the public-good frame.**

Bengaluru's repeated near-Day-Zeros may eventually deliver the latter. Bundelkhand-style invisible drought never will.

The alternative path — and the one that survives federalism and political resistance — is to **build the public-good information layer outside the state, civic-side**, and use it to give the absent stakeholders a voice they currently don't have. That is what `build-plan.md` proposes.

## What surfaces from this stakeholder map

A few practical implications:

1. **Information should be designed for the absent stakeholders first**, not the bureaucrats. A citizen-grade interface in vernacular languages, accessible by SMS/voice, designed for the rural farmer + urban informal user, has very different shape than a portal designed for a CGWB scientist.

2. **Representation in governance structures matters more than data quality.** A perfect aquifer-level dashboard is wasted if women still don't sit on Water User Associations. Capacity-building (Bhujal Jankars, women-led water committees, tribal water-stewardship councils) is co-equal with the information layer.

3. **The cultural-religious bridge is unutilized leverage.** Religious institutions reach populations that no NGO or scheme reaches. A faith-based water-stewardship movement, if seriously pursued, would shift the balance. India's cultural inheritance is the deepest in the world on water; the modern governance frame has never tapped it.

4. **Future generations + non-human users need legal infrastructure.** Intergenerational standing in courts, river personhood revisited, FRA-for-water as a constitutional reform — all are upstream of the everyday decisions the system makes. Activist legal practice + research is part of the build.

5. **The diaspora-capital channel is real and untapped.** A philanthropic India water initiative could plausibly raise meaningful capital from second-generation NRIs who have means and identity but no current vehicle to engage water philanthropy in India. Compare Israel's diaspora playbook.

The full design implication of all this is the build plan in `build-plan.md`.
