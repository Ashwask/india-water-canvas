# The legal vacuum

The constitutional + legal frame around water in India. Why federalism is the deepest structural problem. Why groundwater runs on 1882 law. Why the River Boards Act is dormant. What an FRA-for-water might mean.

## Constitutional architecture

India's Seventh Schedule splits subjects between Centre, States, and Concurrent (both).

- **State List, Entry 17:** "Water, that is to say, water supplies, irrigation and canals, drainage and embankments, water storage and water power subject to the provisions of entry 56 of List I." Water is primarily a State subject.
- **Union List, Entry 56:** "Regulation and development of inter-State rivers and river valleys to the extent to which such regulation and development under the control of the Union is declared by Parliament by law to be expedient in the public interest." Inter-state rivers can be Union-regulated when Parliament declares so. Parliament has been reluctant.
- **Concurrent List:** Forests (17A, moved to Concurrent in 1976), Wildlife (17B), Environment (broadly).

The basic deal: water is primarily State, but inter-state rivers can be Union-regulated. The architecture made sense in 1947 when water was abundant relative to demand. Today the unit of water governance is the basin (often spanning multiple states + sometimes countries). The constitutional unit (state) and the hydrological unit (basin) don't match.

## What "State subject" means in practice

States hold authority over:
- Water resource development (irrigation, hydropower)
- Drinking water supply (urban + rural)
- Sewage and drainage
- Water tariffs (politically captured)
- Allocation among competing uses within state
- State Water Policy
- State Pollution Control Boards (CPCB has limited statutory reach into states)
- Groundwater regulation (varies — TN, Karnataka, MH, Kerala have GW Acts; many states don't)
- Inter-departmental coordination

Centre holds authority over:
- Inter-state rivers via tribunals (Inter-State River Water Disputes Act 1956)
- Centrally Sponsored Schemes — leverage through funding (JJM 50:50, AMRUT, Namami Gange, Atal Bhujal)
- International water treaties (Indus 1960, Ganga 1996, Mahakali 1996, Teesta stalled)
- National scientific agencies (IMD, CWC, CGWB, CPCB) — limited authority to compel state data sharing
- Setting national standards (mostly advisory)
- Ministry of Jal Shakti (formed 2019, merging Water Resources + Drinking Water & Sanitation)

## Three constitutional asymmetries

### 1. Inter-state rivers are litigated, not governed.

Cauvery: Tribunal 1990 → final award 2007 → SC modification 2018 → Cauvery Water Management Authority 2018. Twenty-eight years of dispute with no durable basin governance.

Krishna: KWDT-I 1969, KWDT-II 2010, ongoing SC matters. Telangana-AP carve-out (2014) reopened settled territory.

Godavari: Tribunal 1969, award 1980; Telangana-AP post-2014 reopened.

Mahadayi: Tribunal 2010, ongoing fourteen years.

Ravi-Beas: Tribunal 1986, ongoing thirty-eight years. Punjab Termination of Water Agreements Act 2004 created a separate constitutional crisis.

Yamuna: Multi-state contestation, ongoing, no full tribunal despite 6 states + Delhi involved.

Mahanadi: Tribunal 2018, ongoing.

Each tribunal takes one to two decades. Awards bind in principle, but enforcement requires SC. **No tribunal has produced durable cooperative basin governance.** The Cauvery Water Management Authority is the closest, and it exists because the Supreme Court ordered it, not because basin governance was designed.

### 2. The River Boards Act 1956 has never been invoked.

Centre has statutory power under Entry 56 + River Boards Act to set up river basin boards across states. **In 70 years, never invoked.** The most powerful tool for basin governance lies dormant. Why? Invoking it would require Centre to override state preferences — politically costly, no upside.

### 3. Groundwater is governed by the 1882 Easements Act.

The Indian Easements Act 1882 — colonial-era property law — implies that water beneath the surface belongs to whoever owns the land above. There are no abstraction limits in most of India. There are no permits in most states. There is no community standing.

The **Model Groundwater (Sustainable Management) Bill 2017** circulated; few states adopted. Tamil Nadu Groundwater Act 2003 is one of the strongest state laws; Karnataka 2011, Maharashtra 2009 followed. Most states have nothing.

So a 19th-century property regime is governing a 21st-century crisis. This is, conceptually, the deepest legal gap in Indian water.

## Reform options exist but are politically blocked

**Constitutional amendment moving water to Concurrent List.** Would allow the Centre to legislate national water standards. Politically near-impossible — states would resist any erosion of State subject status.

**Invoking the River Boards Act 1956** for major basins. Statutory tool exists. Would require political consensus that has not materialized in 70 years.

**Inter-State River Water Disputes (Amendment) Act 2019** created a permanent inter-state river dispute tribunal. Helps marginally with the speed problem; doesn't solve cooperative governance.

**National Water Framework Bill** drafted 2016. Aimed to set national principles + state implementation. Stuck politically; never passed.

**Cooperative federalism via Centrally Sponsored Schemes** — current approach. Centre uses funding leverage (JJM, AB, NMCG) to push priorities. States need the money but resist Centre dictating outcomes. Limited reform mechanism.

**National Water Commission** — proposed in 2016 Mihir Shah Committee report (merging CWC + CGWB into a unified National Water Commission). Not implemented.

## How federalism shapes water reality

Three killer implications.

### No national data integration is possible without state cooperation.

Centre cannot compel a state to share data in usable form. NITI Aayog's Composite Water Management Index (CWMI 2018, 2019) ranked states. Rankings embarrassed states. States pushed back. **The index was quietly discontinued.** The structural pattern: when Centre data exposes state failure, the data disappears, not the failure.

JJM water-quality testing data is collected but not aggregated and published nationally. State PCBs report STP performance with lag and partial coverage. Industrial OCEMS data is locked at the agency level. The federation fragmentation is the data fragmentation.

### No basin-scale governance is possible without inter-state cooperation.

Tribunals are slow. River Boards dormant. Voluntary cooperation rare. Cauvery Water Management Authority (2018) is the rare exception, narrow in scope, SC-ordered, not a model that has propagated.

### No national water reform is possible.

Each reform must be implemented state-by-state. Political alignment across 28 states + 8 UTs is improbable. So reform is partial, inconsistent, fragmented. JJM rolls out unevenly. Atal Bhujal touches 7 of 28 states. Namami Gange covers Ganga states only. Each scheme does its piece in its slice.

### States structurally protect their own dysfunction

Punjab paddy + free electricity = catastrophic for the aquifer; politically protected at state level. Maharashtra sugarcane in Marathwada = catastrophic; politically protected by sugar barons → state legislators → CMs. Telangana free power scheme. Tamil Nadu deeper-borewell subsidies. Each state has its own water political settlement that the Centre cannot reach into.

The deeper read: **water as State subject is not just a constitutional fact. It is a political settlement that protects state-level water mismanagement from federal accountability.** Citizens cannot effectively appeal up. The basin cannot self-govern.

## The judicial layer

In the absence of working legislative + executive frameworks, courts have become the de facto enforcers. India's environmental jurisprudence on water is extensive.

- **Vellore Citizens Welfare Forum v Union of India (1996)** — established polluter-pays principle and precautionary principle in Indian law.
- **Subhash Kumar v State of Bihar (1991)** — Article 21 (right to life) includes the right to clean water and air.
- **MC Mehta cases (Ganga, Yamuna, multiple)** — judicial activism on water pollution, ~30 years.
- **National Green Tribunal** (2010+) — fast-track environmental tribunal. Increasingly central for water cases. Limitations: judges + advocates often lack technical capacity, regional NGT benches face local political pressure.
- **Uttarakhand HC 2017** — declared Ganga + Yamuna "living entities" with personhood. Supreme Court stayed within months.
- **Free Prior Informed Consent** under Forest Rights Act 2006 — mandates tribal consent for forest land diversion. Not strictly applied to water projects.

What's emerging globally but not yet in India:

- **Intergenerational legal standing** (Netherlands Urgenda 2019 climate case; German Constitutional Court 2021 climate ruling holding the state to a duty toward future generations).
- **River personhood** (New Zealand Whanganui River 2017; Ecuador's Rights of Nature in constitution; Bangladesh High Court 2019 declared all rivers as legal persons).

India tried river personhood in 2017 and it was overturned. The legal frame remains: water is a resource and a right, never a being.

## Treaties and transboundary water

International legal layer:

**Indus Waters Treaty (1960)** — India + Pakistan + World Bank. Allocates eastern rivers (Ravi, Beas, Sutlej) to India and western rivers (Indus, Jhelum, Chenab) to Pakistan, with India holding limited use rights on western rivers. Built around glacier-fed flows. Indus headwater glaciers have lost 20-40% mass since 1960. Treaty has no climate-renegotiation clause. India suspended September 2025 post-Pahalgam attack — the legal frame is broken; the climate frame was already broken.

**Ganga Treaty (1996)** — India + Bangladesh on Farakka Barrage water sharing. Lean-season minimum flows. Periodic stress; no climate-update clause.

**Mahakali Treaty (1996)** — India + Nepal. Pancheshwar dam dispute, decades of stalled implementation.

**Teesta** — India + Bangladesh negotiations stalled since 2011 due to West Bengal opposition. Lean-season flow has fallen materially; downstream Bangladesh agriculture suffers.

**Brahmaputra** — India + China have **no treaty**. China is building large dams on Yarlung Tsangpo. India and Bangladesh downstream. China shares hydrological data only seasonally, mostly for flood warning, with delays.

Climate is rewriting all four. Indian water diplomacy has not adapted.

## What an "FRA for water" might look like

The Forest Rights Act 2006 took 60 years of post-Independence forest governance to recognize that the people who lived in forests had prior rights to them. It transferred legal standing from the state to the community for forest commons. Imperfect, gameable, but conceptually real.

Water has nothing equivalent.

Surface water is "state property" in most legal interpretations, allocated by Irrigation Departments. Groundwater is private under the 1882 Easements Act. Customary water rights — village tank irrigation, springshed stewardship, riverine fishing rights, river-bed seasonal cultivation — exist nowhere in formal law.

A **Water Rights Act** that mirrors FRA would do something like:

- **Recognize community rights** over water bodies and aquifers — Community Water Resources analogous to Community Forest Resources.
- **Require participatory aquifer management plans** before bulk extraction.
- **Give downstream villages legal standing** in upstream allocation decisions.
- **Force consent** for diversion projects through tribal Gram Sabhas in scheduled areas.
- **Recognize riparian community rights** to fishing, sand, and seasonal floodplain cultivation.
- **Force documentation** of customary water knowledge and institutions.
- **Establish independent dispute resolution** at the basin scale, not at the political-state scale.

Why hasn't it happened?

- **Farmers' lobby** would resist any limit on extraction.
- **Industry** would resist any community veto.
- **Urban water utilities** would resist anything that gives rural watersheds a claim on their imports (Bengaluru drinking Cauvery, Mumbai drinking Bhatsa, Delhi drinking Yamuna upstream).
- **State water bureaucracies** would resist anything that gives the basin standing relative to the state.
- **No political coalition** for this exists.

The void is the deepest single legal gap in Indian water. Filling it is plausibly the single most consequential constitutional reform that's not on anyone's agenda.

## What "build outside the state" looks like in legal terms

If federalism makes top-down national reform near-impossible, the alternative — civic-side build — is also legally constrained.

- **Right to Information Act 2005** is generic, slow, and increasingly captured by exemptions. Useful for one-off queries; inadequate for continuous data infrastructure.
- **No statutory right to water information** exists. Water-specific data access is at the discretion of the source agency.
- **No data protection regime** covers sensor data or citizen science. Independent measurement is in legal grey territory.
- **Polluter privacy + industrial confidentiality** protect bad actors. CETP discharge data is treated as confidential.
- **Anti-defamation + cyber-libel laws** are routinely used against journalists who expose water mismanagement. Sand-mining journalists have been killed (Sandeep Kothari 2015 + others).
- **FCRA restrictions** since 2020 have constrained foreign philanthropic funding into Indian civic infrastructure. Domestic capital is the realistic path.

A civic-side water information platform would need to design legal posture carefully:

- Use only public data + citizen science + properly licensed satellite data.
- Build redundancy across legal jurisdictions for hosting + backups.
- Establish legal infrastructure (in-house counsel + retainer + insurance) for adversarial pressure.
- Maintain transparency about methodology + sources to defuse defamation claims.
- Avoid FCRA exposure where domestic anchor capital is available.
- Engage NGT proactively on data integrity issues to build judicial backstop.

The legal vacuum cuts both ways: it makes top-down reform impossible AND makes bottom-up civic infrastructure legally fragile. Both need work.

## Implications for the build

The legal frame is one of the load-bearing reasons that **`build-plan.md`** structures itself the way it does:

- Civic-side, not state-side.
- Federated alliance, not centralized foundation.
- Diversified funding (no FCRA single-source dependency).
- Decentralized hosting (no single jurisdictional choke point).
- Long-term policy advocacy (Phase 8) rather than short-term reform asks.
- Legal infrastructure built in from Phase 0.

Reforming Indian water is not, in the first instance, a technical problem. It is a constitutional problem dressed up as a technical problem.
