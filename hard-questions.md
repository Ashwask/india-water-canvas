# Hard questions

Twelve questions this folder forces but does not resolve. Each is structural rather than tactical — the kind of question that, answered honestly, would shift the trajectory of the build sketched in `build-plan.md`. Each ends with a one-sentence test: what evidence would change the answer.

These are not rhetorical. They are open. The honest posture is that the folder's argument may be partially wrong, and the questions are where the argument is most exposed.

## 1. Is the binding constraint capital, talent, or institutional permission?

If India already has more water data than is widely understood (`data-and-groundtruth.md`), and the public-good information system that would integrate it is technically tractable at ₹500-1,000 cr over 10 years (`funders-ecosystem.md`), then why has no one built it? Three candidate constraints. **Capital** — the rupee envelope exists in aggregate (Arghyam endowment + Rainmatter + Tata + diaspora pools + climate finance) but the vehicle to receive it does not. **Talent** — the joint hydrological + software + civic + legal capacity to staff a federated data trust is rare; perhaps 50-100 people in India today have all four, distributed across IndiaWaterPortal, WELL Labs, ATREE, ACWADAM, IIT-B CWRR, and a few state agencies, none under one institutional roof. **Institutional permission** — for a Section 8 / Section 25 to receive state-agency data feeds, build cross-walks with CWC + CGWB + CPCB, and operate at SPCB-adjacent altitude requires a deputy-secretary-level partnership that has never been signed. Which of the three is binding? *What would change my mind: a credible founding-team announcement (talent-adequate) backed by anchor capital (capital-adequate) that gets blocked at MoU stage by Jal Shakti — the proof the binding constraint is institutional, not the other two.*

## 2. Is the JJM tap-coverage KPI a public good or a political artefact?

The Jal Jeevan Mission has reported 81%+ rural household tap coverage on a daily-updated dashboard. CAG audits 2022 + 2024 documented definition slippage on "functional household tap connection" — measured at installation, not at continuous service. Field-level estimates of actual functionality cluster around 40-55% under post-warranty O&M. The KPI is celebrated; the contradiction with FTK water-quality testing is politically sensitive (`data-and-groundtruth.md`). The question: is this a KPI the public-good frame can rescue with better measurement, or is it a KPI whose political construction is fundamentally hostile to honest measurement? If the latter, the entire DDWS scheme architecture post-2028 is built on a number that cannot survive scrutiny. **What would change my mind: a state-level (not national) JJM functionality study by a credible academic + civic partnership showing convergence between dashboard and ground-truth — proof the gap is fixable rather than structural. None has been published.**

## 3. Why does India price water near zero while pricing carbon and air increasingly?

Free agricultural electricity to ~30M tubewells removes the price signal that would expose abstraction. Surface water tariffs are politically captured at far below cost recovery in every state. Pollution discharge has no Pigouvian price; polluters pay nothing material. Meanwhile carbon pricing through PAT + the proposed Indian Carbon Market is moving forward, and ambient air quality has become politically salient through CPCB AQI dashboards under urban middle-class pressure. The contrast is striking — water has *more* aggregate physical risk than air or carbon, and *less* political pressure for pricing reform. The political economy explanation: carbon and air affect the urban middle class directly; water (especially groundwater) affects rural and informal users whose political weight is lower. The question: what changes the settlement? *What would change my mind: a single state government — Punjab, Tamil Nadu, Karnataka — passing a credible groundwater pricing reform with measurement infrastructure and political survival past one election cycle. None looks plausible in the 2026-30 horizon.*

## 4. Is FRA-for-water plausible in a 5-year horizon, or is it a 50-year project?

The Easements Act 1882 still treats groundwater as an appendage of land — surface owner has unrestricted abstraction rights below their plot. The Forest Rights Act 2006 created community resource rights for forest commons, ending 150 years of state-monopoly assumption. A water analogue — community resource rights over springsheds, basin commons, traditional water bodies, micro-aquifers — is conceptually defensible (`legal-vacuum.md`) but legislatively absent. The question: is the FRA-for-water push a 5-year legislative project that needs a clear bill draft + civil-society coalition + sympathetic government, or is it a 50-year cultural project that requires the political settlement around water itself to shift before legal reform becomes possible? Most reform arguments collapse the distinction; the difference matters for sequencing. **What would change my mind: a credible coalition (FES + ACWADAM + a state government partner + a parliamentarian + 2-3 funder anchors) publishing a draft National Water Framework Bill or an FRA-amendment with explicit water provisions, surviving committee referral. Until that happens, the prudent posture is 50-year.**

## 5. What does an audit muscle for water look like — civic, captured-state, or hybrid?

The verification layer in `gaps.md` requires an audit muscle that holds the state honest on its own data. Two paths. **Civic-side** — a federation of independent labs + citizen-science network + standing legal capacity, modelled loosely on Election Commission observer architecture or financial auditing. **Captured-state** — pick one State PCB, fund its institutional reform to NABL + NGT-defendable measurement standards, demonstrate the model, force replication through mimetic pressure. The civic path scales reach; the captured-state path scales credibility. The question: is the right move to attempt both in parallel, or does the architecture force a choice? Resource constraints favour the choice. *What would change my mind: a working partnership where one State PCB (Karnataka, Maharashtra, or Tamil Nadu are the most plausible) commits to civic co-monitoring and publishes the comparison readings — the existence proof that hybrid is viable. None visible today.*

## 6. Basin, state, watershed, or city — what is the right unit?

Every Indian water framework chooses one unit and treats the others as residual. Basin authorities (1956 River Boards Act, dormant) treat states as data providers. State schemes (JJM, AMRUT, state missions) treat watersheds as project sites. Watershed development (PMKSY-WDC) treats cities and basin allocation as out-of-scope. City water (AMRUT, Smart Cities, ULB billing) treats the basin as an assumed input. The question: is the right architecture one unit dominant + others subordinate, or genuinely multi-scalar with co-equal authorities at each layer? The 2030 Water Resources Group's multi-stakeholder platform is the closest current attempt at multi-scalar; its outcomes are mixed. **What would change my mind: a working basin-scale governance instance — Cauvery is the closest living candidate via the Cauvery Water Management Authority — surviving 5+ years with measurable allocation discipline. Even Cauvery is litigation-driven rather than governance-driven, which suggests the basin unit is institutionally underdeveloped.**

## 7. What is the actual cost of metering groundwater at pump-head across 6,000+ over-exploited blocks?

The single most important missing measurement in Indian water (`gaps.md` Layer 1). Estimates vary by two orders of magnitude depending on assumptions. **Floor estimate**: ~₹50 cr (smart-meter retrofit on the 1-2% of pumps in the worst-case blocks, sample-based). **Ceiling estimate**: ~₹15,000-20,000 cr (full metering across all ~30M tubewells nationally with telemetry). The decision-grade middle — ~₹2,000-5,000 cr capex + ~₹500-800 cr/year opex covering 5-10M pumps in priority blocks with sample-based extrapolation — is what an honest cost model would land on. The question: who bears the cost? *Extractor* (politically infeasible — would breach the free-agri-electricity settlement); *state* (unlikely under current state-finance pressure); *ratepayer through tariff reform* (depends on Q3 above); *philanthropic + civic for the priority subset* (plausible if costed at ₹100-200 cr civic-side seed). **What would change my mind: a single state pilot (Andhra Pradesh, Punjab, or Karnataka) with metering at 50,000+ pumps surviving 3 years with publicly accessible data — proof the political economy can be navigated at sub-state scale.**

## 8. What is the analogue to cyclones that builds the water constituency?

India built strong cyclone information after 1999 Odisha (~10,000 dead) when the political cost of failure became acute. AQI dashboards followed Delhi air after middle-class campaigning made it politically central. COVID dashboards were built in weeks under acute crisis. Water has had its acute moments — Chennai Day Zero 2019, Bengaluru near-Day-Zero 2024-25, the Sundarbans Aila + Bulbul + Yaas displacement, Kashmir 2014 floods, Chamoli + South Lhonak GLOFs. None has produced the constituency that translates into honest data infrastructure. The question: is there an analogue event waiting in the next 5-10 years that would deliver the constituency, and can civic-side actors prepare to channel it? Or is chronic water doomed to slow-burn opacity because no acute event aligns interests at scale? **What would change my mind: a Bengaluru full-Day-Zero in summer 2026 or 2027 that mobilises sustained urban middle-class campaigning for ward-scale supply transparency, surviving past the immediate crisis. The signal: does the campaign fizzle in 6 months or build the institutional muscle?**

## 9. Can the corporate water-positive claim ecosystem survive a single high-profile audit failure?

`funders-ecosystem.md` flags ₹800-1,200 cr/year of CSR water-positive claims potentially fictional offset accounting. The audit cost to verify the envelope is ~₹30-50 cr. The political infrastructure to support an audit case — investigative journalism, legal capacity to defend against defamation, regulatory body willing to receive findings — exists in scattered form. The question: is anyone preparing the case? Coca-Cola Kaladera + Plachimada precedents show the corporate-side defence playbook (questioning methodology, settlement to silence, employee deployment of opacity). The civic-side equivalent would be sustained, methodologically tight, peer-reviewed, legally defensible — not episodic activism. **What would change my mind: a credible coalition (CSE + IndiaSpend + an academic centre + a litigating-class NGO + funder anchor) announcing a multi-year corporate water-positive audit project. The closest current activity is sectoral — CSE's *Down to Earth* coverage — not target-specific. The targeted case is the one that would shift the equilibrium.**

## 10. What does a non-state river-basin authority look like?

The Murray-Darling Basin Authority in Australia is the international comparison most cited in Indian water reform discourse. It is statutory + state-funded; not a non-state model. The question: could a *civic-side* basin authority — funded philanthropically, holding open-data infrastructure for a single basin, building governance recommendations the state can adopt or reject — be a viable institutional form in India? The legal posture is non-trivial: the River Boards Act 1956 reserves basin authority to the Centre + state framework; a Section 8 vehicle could not have statutory authority but could have research + monitoring + recommendation authority. WELL Labs' basin work is the closest current analogue at sub-national scale. The question: is this the architecture for ten priority basins, or is the right path a state-and-civic hybrid where the civic body has data + recommendations and the state body has decisions? *What would change my mind: a basin where civic-side data + recommendations have visibly shaped state allocation decisions over a 3-5 year window. The existence proof has not been published; the signal would be a Cauvery sub-basin or a Krishna sub-basin where civic data was demonstrably used.*

## 11. Is the canon enough, or does India need a new water-civilization narrative?

The cultural canon — *Dying Wisdom* (Agarwal + Narain 1997), *Aaj Bhi Khare Hain Talab* (Mishra 1994), *The Wells of Memory* (Ramesh 2024), Veditum's river archive, regional canons in Marathi + Tamil + Kannada + Bengali + Hindi — is rich, deep, and broadly accessible to those who seek it. The reach is the question. Most urban schoolchildren cannot describe their city's water source. Hydrological imagination is a civic-literacy gap (`gaps.md` Layer 7). The canon was written for an India that no longer exists — pre-climate-non-stationarity, pre-mass-urbanisation, pre-Day-Zero. Does India need a new water-civilization narrative for the climate era — written for a citizenry that has never lived through stationary hydrology — and if so, who writes it? *What would change my mind: a 21st-century canonical work that achieves Mishra-or-Agarwal-level penetration in vernacular Indian-school curricula and urban public discourse. The closest current candidate is Ramesh's *Wells of Memory*; it is excellent but not yet that. The narrative gap may need a generation, not a decade.*

## 12. Who anchors the build, and is one anchor resilience or fragility?

`funders-ecosystem.md` ends on the anchor question. Arghyam is the only water-pure philanthropy at meaningful scale. ATE Chandra is the only at-scale water-bodies operator. The plausible anchor profiles for a ₹500-1,000 cr build over 10 years are: (a) Arghyam scale-up with explicit public-good information mandate; (b) ATE Chandra + Tata Trusts + Premji co-anchor; (c) new pooled vehicle modelled on India Climate Collaborative; (d) diaspora capital pool; (e) Rainmatter as catalytic seeding; (f) hybrid Section 8 + government co-funding. The question: is one anchor sufficient, or does a public-good build require *at least three uncorrelated funder anchors* to survive officer transfers + political turnover + funder-strategy shifts over a 10-year horizon? Foundations have life-cycle pivots; one anchor is structurally fragile. **What would change my mind: a credible co-anchor commitment — two funders publicly co-pledging multi-decade capital with explicit non-substitution clauses (i.e., one cannot replace the other). No such pair exists today; the build proposed in `build-plan.md` is therefore vulnerable at exactly the institutional layer it most depends on.**

## What this file does not resolve

The questions are honest. The answers are not in this folder. Some are in `build-plan.md` (the construction logic that anticipates which questions are decisive). Some are in `imagination.md` (the deepest, slowest layer). Most are open questions that any serious build must hold open as it proceeds — the questions that *should* keep the founding team awake, because resolving them correctly is what separates a public-good build from a vanity project.

The right test for this list, ten years on, is whether the questions still feel hard. If they look easy in retrospect, either the build worked or the diagnosis was wrong. Both outcomes are interesting; the unstable one is the build limping along while the questions stay open. That is the failure mode worth most actively guarding against.
