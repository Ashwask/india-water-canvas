# India water — the landscape

The factual ground. What is happening, where, at what scale. Subsequent files build on this.

## Resource and scale

India holds roughly 4% of the world's freshwater and supports roughly 18% of the world's population. The ratio is the headline.

**Rainfall.** Average ~1,170 mm per year. But ~75% arrives in four monsoon months, and roughly half of annual rainfall falls in fewer than 100 hours per year. India is rainfall-abundant; it is timing-poor.

**Surface water.** Roughly 70% of usable surface water sits in the Ganga–Brahmaputra–Meghna system. Peninsular rivers — Cauvery, Krishna, Godavari, Pennar — are climatically stressed and over-allocated. Trans-Himalayan rivers (Indus tributaries) are glacier-fed and shifting.

**Groundwater.** India is the world's largest groundwater user, accounting for roughly 25% of global extraction. Around 60% of irrigation and 85% of rural drinking water comes from groundwater. By the latest CGWB Ground Water Estimation (2023), about 17% of assessment blocks are classified "over-exploited" — extraction has crossed 100% of annual recharge — and another large share is "critical" or "semi-critical." The trend is one direction.

**Cryosphere.** Himalayan glaciers feed the Indus, Ganga, and Brahmaputra basins. Mass loss across the Hindu Kush Himalayan range is documented by ICIMOD as accelerating. Long-term basin water security depends on the cryosphere; cryospheric monitoring within India is paper-thin.

**Springs.** Critical in Himalayan, Northeast, and Western Ghats geographies. Sikkim's spring atlas (2008+) documented that around half of mapped springs were going seasonal. Meghalaya, Uttarakhand, and other hill states show similar patterns. Outside Sikkim and Meghalaya, springs are largely uninventoried.

**Per-capita availability** has fallen from ~5,177 m³/year (1951) to ~1,486 m³/year (2021). Below 1,700 m³ is "water stressed"; below 1,000 m³ is "water scarce." India crosses the scarcity threshold by 2030 on current trajectory. The averages hide brutal asymmetry.

## Ten stress fronts

Where the urgency is.

1. **Groundwater depletion belt.** Punjab, Haryana, western UP, Rajasthan parts, Telangana, Tamil Nadu. Punjab water tables fall ~0.5–1 m/year in central districts. Some blocks are now 200m+ wells. Recharge would take centuries.
2. **Drought belts.** Marathwada, Vidarbha, Bundelkhand, Saurashtra, Rayalaseema, Anantapur. Six-out-of-ten drought years in some belts. Water trains restarted in Marathwada in 2024.
3. **Urban "Day Zero" cities.** Bengaluru hit Day Zero in March 2024 — tankers ran dry, apartments paid ₹50,000+/month for water. Most middle-class residents barely noticed because tankers + private borewells absorbed the shock. Chennai 2019 was a practice run. Hyderabad, Delhi, Shimla on the same trajectory.
4. **River health collapse.** Yamuna in Delhi: BOD 50+ mg/L (limit: 3 mg/L). Cauvery (lower), Sabarmati (lean season), Musi (Hyderabad), Mithi (Mumbai). CPCB's polluted river stretch list has hovered around 311.
5. **Wetland loss.** Around 30% of India's wetland area lost in four decades (NWI/SAC analysis, dated). 80+ Ramsar sites; protection uneven. Bengaluru: ~1,500 tanks in 1960; ~200 left. Hyderabad similar. Chennai 60% built on tank-bed.
6. **Coastal salinity / sea level.** Sundarbans has lost ~210 sq km to sea since 1969. Krishna-Godavari delta sinking. Cauvery delta and Kerala backwater wells turning brackish. The first wave of Indian climate refugees is coastal.
7. **Flood + drought in same basin.** Bihar floods July-September every year while drinking water is arsenic-laced and dry-season scarcity is severe. Assam: annual Brahmaputra floods + arsenic + iron + fluoride. Kerala: 2018, 2019, 2024 mudslides + summer drought + saline intrusion. Bundelkhand: drought 6/10 years + flash floods between.
8. **Inter-state water conflicts.** Cauvery (KA-TN-KL-PY, tribunal 1990 → award 2007 → SC 2018 → CWMA 2018, 28 years). Krishna (MH-KA-AP-TG, KWDT-II 2010, ongoing). Godavari (1980 award, post-2014 reopened). Mahadayi (2010, ongoing). Yamuna (multi-state, no full tribunal). Mahanadi (2018). Ravi-Beas (1986, ongoing).
9. **Water quality / mass poisoning.** Arsenic: ~50 million exposed in Ganga-Brahmaputra plain (West Bengal, Bihar, UP, Assam, Jharkhand). Fluoride: 200+ districts, peninsular hard-rock geology, ~60M+ exposed. Nitrate: agricultural runoff. Uranium: 16 states (Duke 2018 study). Heavy metals: industrial belts (Kanpur tanneries, Tirupur textile, Patancheru pharma). Microplastics: every river tested has them. PFAS: effectively zero data.
10. **Springshed decline.** Himalayan + NE villages losing perennial springs. Sikkim documented 50% of springs going seasonal (1990-2010). Meghalaya similar. Living root bridges (centuries-old water infrastructure) tied to a community of practice dying with elders.

## Climate has already broken stationarity

Every institutional water plan in India is built on stationarity — historical means as predictors of the future. They no longer predict.

- Monsoon variability is now ±30% across years and rising.
- ~75% of annual rain now arrives in fewer, more intense events. "Cloudbursts" used to be rare; now seasonal.
- Bay of Bengal is ~1.5°C warmer than 30 years ago — more cyclone energy. 2024 alone: Cyclones Remal, Asna, Dana, Fengal.
- Himalayan cryosphere is leaking. ICIMOD has flagged 200+ dangerous glacial lakes. South Lhonak (Sikkim, October 2023) blew through a Teesta-III dam in 90 minutes, killing ~100. It was forewarned in 2018-2020 academic literature and acted on by no one.
- Sea level rising on Indian coasts at ~3-4 mm/year, slightly faster in some sub-regions.

The Cauvery 2018 SC allocation, the Krishna KWDT-II, the Indus Waters Treaty (1960, India suspended September 2025 post-Pahalgam), the Brahmaputra (no treaty with China, Yarlung Tsangpo dam construction upstream), the Teesta (stalled with Bangladesh since 2011) — every existing water deal was signed under stationarity assumptions that are already wrong. Within 20 years every major water deal in India will need renegotiation under stress. There is no institutional muscle for that.

## The buffer is gone

Healthy aquifers, tank cascades, and wetlands buffer rainfall volatility. Drought becomes survivable; floods get absorbed. India has spent its buffer over 40 years — paving wetlands, draining aquifers, breaking tank cascades. Climate volatility now hits a system with no shock absorbers. Every flood and every drought is amplified by the lost buffer.

This is not strictly a climate problem. It is the collision of climate stress with degraded buffers. The Bengaluru floods of 2022-2024 are not freak events; they are paved-over tank cascades asserting themselves. The flood map of any Indian city is its lost-wetlands map.

## Vanishing geography

India is losing several entire water civilizations simultaneously, slowly enough that no generation experiences the full loss.

- Bengaluru: ~1,500 tanks → ~200, IT parks built on tank beds.
- Chennai: 60% of city on what were tank beds; 2015 floods were the tanks asserting themselves.
- Sundarbans: −210 sq km to sea since 1969; salinity 100+ km inland in some channels.
- Himalayan springs: 50% perennial → seasonal in Sikkim study; Meghalaya similar.
- Kashmir's Dal Lake: 22 sq km in 1980 → ~10 sq km now, half-eutrophic.
- Vernacular water vocabulary — *talab, kere, ahar, kuhl, zing, bawdi, eri, oran, johad, kund, baoli, naula, dhara* — disappearing as elders pass and English/Hindi standardizes. Each was institutional knowledge embedded in language.

## The intersections

Treating flood, drought, water quality as separate problems with separate budgets is operationally lethal. They are one system in collapse.

- Bihar: floods + arsenic-laced groundwater + dry-season scarcity, same year, same villages.
- Assam: Brahmaputra floods + iron/arsenic/fluoride + sanitation-driven disease, perennially.
- Kerala: monsoon mudslides + saline intrusion + summer drought in Idukki/Wayanad.
- Bundelkhand: drought 6/10 years + flash floods + endemic fluoride.
- Coastal AP, TN, OD: cyclone-flood-salinity-fluoride stack.

The same geography that floods is the geography that doesn't have water 9 months a year and is the geography where what water exists is poisoned.

## The asymmetric failure

The costs of breakdown are not random. They fall predictably on:

- Smallholder farmers (60% of users; minimal voice)
- Urban informal settlements (most water-precarious; zero representation)
- Tribal and Adivasi communities (live on watersheds; allocate nothing)
- Women (~80% of household water labour; ~14% of agricultural land ownership)
- Children (35% under-5 stunted; WASH absence drives school dropout)
- Dalit communities (caste-based water exclusion persistent despite 1955/1989 laws; water access is one of the most common village-level atrocity triggers per NCRB)
- Sanitation workers (manual scavenger deaths in hundreds annually, almost entirely Dalit)
- Downstream users (no principled compensation framework)
- Future generations (aquifers drained in one generation that took 1000 years to fill; discount rates value future at zero)
- Non-human users (rivers, wetlands, fish, deltas — no legal personhood after Uttarakhand 2017 SC overturn)

The system is not failing randomly. It is failing in a predicted direction. The deepest reframe of this landscape is: **the system is not broken — it is working as designed, for someone.**

## The institutional vacuum

- No working river basin organization with teeth, anywhere. Cauvery Water Management Authority works because the Supreme Court ordered it.
- River Boards Act 1956 has never been invoked in 70 years.
- Groundwater is governed by the 1882 Easements Act — surface landowner owns water beneath. The Model Groundwater Bill 2017 is mostly unadopted.
- Water is a State subject under the Seventh Schedule. Centre is advisory. Inter-state issues go to tribunals (decade-long), then SC.
- Urban water utilities are politically captured. Tariffs are political. Non-revenue water is 30-50%. Metering is incomplete.

Reform options exist on paper — National Water Framework Bill (drafted 2016, never passed), River Boards Act invocation, ISWDA 2019 amendment (permanent tribunal), constitutional amendment moving water to Concurrent List. Each is politically blocked.

## The information vacuum

Citizens cannot answer the basic question "how is my water doing right now?" with public information. Real-time aquifer monitoring at usable resolution does not exist. Sewage discharge data is private. Industrial discharge data is private. Water-quality data is monthly grab-samples publishable with 6-12 month lag. Climate scenarios are not integrated into existing inter-state allocation. Cross-agency stitching across IMD + CWC + CGWB + CPCB + ISRO + state PCBs is broken — six different ministries, six different schemas, six different update cycles.

The pattern: India has plenty of water data. It does not have a public-good information system. The information vacuum is the institutional vacuum's reflection.

## Why the system is in this shape

Layered reasons explored across `legal-vacuum.md`, `data-and-groundtruth.md`, and `gaps.md`. The deepest structural read:

> **Opacity is the policy.** Free electricity for agricultural pumps is a vote bank — metering = political suicide. So abstraction data doesn't get collected. Without abstraction data, no water-information system can be honest. Inter-state disputes are politically managed; common-floor data would constrain political latitude. Water-borne disease data is politically inconvenient because it exposes scheme failure (JJM, Swachh Bharat). The opacity is the political settlement.

`hard-questions.md` lays out the structural questions this landscape forces. `build-plan.md` describes one concrete bet on what to build in response.
